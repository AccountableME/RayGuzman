The Work of the Board

Organizations Rise to the Level of the Questions Their Directors Ask

A reflection on a single sentence and what it demands of the people in the room.

← Thoughts from within the Boardroom

I read the line a couple of years ago, and it has stayed with me: organizations rise to the level of the questions being asked by their board directors.

At first, it sounds like a tidy aphorism, the kind that fits on a slide and quickly loses force. But if you sit with it for a while, it becomes more demanding. It relocates responsibility. When an organization is underperforming its potential, the comfortable explanation is that management fell short. The harder question is whether the board ever raised the ceiling in the first place.

Why questions, and not answers, are the board’s real instrument

Directors do not run the company, and we should not try to. We cannot match management’s grasp of daily operations, and we should be cautious around any director who pretends otherwise. What we do control is subtler and, over time, more powerful: the questions that get asked in the room where the organization explains itself.

A question is an instrument of attention. Whatever a board chooses to ask about, management learns to prepare for. Whatever the board never asks, management learns it can neglect without consequence. Over enough cycles, the organization quietly reshapes itself around the contours of the board’s curiosity. Ask only about this quarter, and you will get an organization superb at this quarter and weak beyond it. Ask about the decade, the second-order effects, the assumption nobody has tested, and the organization slowly builds the muscles required to answer.

Whatever the board never asks, management learns it can safely neglect.

The difference between a good question and a comfortable one

Not every question raises the ceiling. Some quietly lower it. The comfortable question confirms what everyone already believes and lets the meeting end on time. It performs diligence without risking discovery. “Are we on track against the plan?” is usually a comfortable question because it is often an invitation to say yes.

The questions that elevate an organization tend to share a few traits:

  • They are answerable, but not easily. A good question can be answered with work. A bad one is either trivial or unanswerable, and both let management off the hook.

  • They expose assumptions rather than confirm them. “What would have to be true for this plan to fail?” moves a room in a way “will this plan succeed?” rarely does.

  • They are willing to be unwelcome. The most useful question is often the one everyone in the room hoped would not come up.

  • They invite a real answer, not a defense. Tone matters. A question asked to corner someone produces theater. A question asked to understand produces information.

The trap of the impressive question

There is one failure mode worth naming because experienced directors can fall into it. It is the question asked to show how much the asker already knows rather than to learn something useful. It may sound impressive in the moment, but it moves nothing forward. Looking insightful is not the same as being useful. A board where people compete to seem clever produces noise, and management gradually learns to manage the performance instead of dealing with the issue.

The discipline is to ask in service of the organization rather than in service of your own standing in the room. That is harder than it sounds, especially for accomplished people who arrived at the board because they are used to being one of the smartest voices at the table.

What this asks of you, practically

If the sentence is true, a few obligations follow, and none of them are abstract:

  1. Prepare to ask, not just to listen. Walking in with two or three questions that could change a decision is real preparation. Reading the deck is the minimum, not the work.

  2. Protect the person who asks the hard one. If raising an uncomfortable question carries social cost, the board has capped its own ceiling. The chair’s job includes making the unwelcome question safe to ask.

  3. Watch the questions you are not asking. The gaps in a board’s curiosity are where the next problem may be compounding. Periodically audit what has fallen off the agenda and ask why.

  4. Measure the board by what it surfaced. A board that never changed a decision, clarified a risk, or improved an assumption through its questions may be less effective than its members believe.

The sentence is generous in one respect. It says the level is not fixed. Organizations can rise, but someone in the room has to insist on it. That someone is supposed to be the board. The ceiling of the enterprise is set, quietly and continuously, by the quality of the questions its directors are willing to ask and keep asking after the easy answers run out.
